WHY A CIRCLE HERE HAS 256 STEPS

Not 360. Three hundred and sixty is a human convention with no factor of two in it worth having, and every time an angle ran off the end you would need a division to bring it back. With 256 an angle wraps in one instruction, and the quarter you are in is a shift:

and  ax, 255      ; 255 = 11111111b. Any angle at all -> 0..255
mov  cl, 6
shr  bx, cl       ; BX = which quarter, 0..3   (that is a/64)
and  ax, 63       ; AX = how far into it       (that is a mod 64)

Those last two lines are a quotient and a remainder, from a shift and a mask. AND with a mask of all ones IS the remainder of a division by a power of two — not an approximation of it, the same answer, in three clocks where DIV wants a hundred and sixty. It only works when the divisor is a power of two, which is why every constant in the file is one.

SAR IS NOT SHR, AND IT IS NOT A STYLE CHOICE

Half the sines in the table are negative, and the two shift-right instructions disagree completely about what that means. Take −8, written as a byte for legibility:

-8 = 11111000b

SAR 1  ->  11111100b  =   -4      correct
SHR 1  ->  01111100b  = +124      a large positive number

SAR copies the top bit into itself instead of letting a zero in, so the sign survives and the shift really is a divide. Use the other one here and every point below the axis is flung to the far side of the screen. One letter. And SAR rounds down, towards minus infinity, not towards zero — which is where one-pixel errors come from.

SEVENTY, IN THREE SHIFTS AND TWO ADDS

Frame 4 is the lesson paying a debt. The ring costs two IMULs a point, and IMUL is 128 clocks on an 8086 where ADD is 3. But the radius is 70, and 70 in binary is 1000110b — three bits set, at 64, 4 and 2:

n * 70  =  n*64 + n*4 + n*2

256*64 = 16384
256*4  =  1024      16384 + 1024 = 17408
256*2  =   512      17408 +  512 = 17920   = 256 * 70

Three shifts, two adds, about 30 clocks. And it is exact — nothing is rounded and nothing is approximated, which is what frame 4 exists to show: the two rings are drawn on top of each other, red first and white over it, and every one of the 128 coordinates is compared. If a single one disagreed the top of the screen would turn red.

The pixel address goes the same way. A row is 320 bytes, and 320 is 256 + 64, so y*320 is two shifts and an add and the last multiply in the program is gone.

THE NUMBER THAT WAS WRONG FOR YEARS

The table in SINT.INC was generated by MKSIN.C, which is in this bundle to read rather than to build — it needs a compiler and floating point, and neither is in here. That is the arrangement the whole repository runs on: today's tools prepare the data, and the 8086 is the runtime, never the toolchain.

The generated table and the hand-made one carried by three of my shipped games agree on 64 values out of 65. They disagree at index 26, where the true value is 152.4990 — four ten-thousandths below the rounding boundary. A hand rounded up. It is an error of one part in 256 of a sine, it is invisible, and it is in all three games identically, which tells you it was worked out once and copied twice.

CHANGE SOMETHING

The assembler is in the tab with you, which is the point of this page rather than a video of it:

EDIT TURN.ASM
MAKE
RUN

RAD is 70. Try 40, try 95. Above 127 the ring turns inside out, and the comment beside IMUL says exactly why: sine times 256 times 128 is 32768, which is negative in sixteen bits. Reload the page and everything is back as it was, so there is nothing you can break.

LESSON 16 — THE GROUND BECOMES A SURFACE

click the black screen first to grab the keyboard · this lesson wants a real keyboard · back to the arcade
Type MAKE, then RUN. Space takes off. Arrows fly it — down is nose up. Dive into the ground and see what it does now.

THE PAINTER

Every square is filled, and they are drawn far to near. The near hill covers the far one because it was painted later — nothing is tested at all. Last lesson's 320-byte skyline is gone.

THE CRASH

A filled hillside is not a floor. Fly into it and the flight ends: one byte stops the model, the camera steps back, and SPACE puts another aeroplane in the air.

THE CIRCLE

The view reaches twice as far, and turning the old square block forty five degrees overflowed a 16-bit word. So the view is a circle now — and the horizon stays put as you turn.

THE PAINTER IS AN ORDER, NOT AN ALGORITHM

Lesson 15 walked its table of squares from the nearest outwards and asked a 320-byte array, pixel by pixel, whether anything nearer was in the way. This lesson walks the same table from the other end, fills every square, and lets the paint do the hiding. The whole change in the loop is one instruction:

lesson 15:   mov [ordk],0          ...   inc word [ordk]
lesson 16:   mov [ordk],the last   ...   dec word [ordk]

You do not pay for hiding. You get it with the colour. What you do pay for is the filling — and the filler the game uses, SCANPOLY, does one multiply and one divide for every edge on every row.

THE SEA CAME OUT IN WAVES

The best bug in the lesson. The sea sits at height nought and is flat — but on the screen it was blue and full of hills. The code kept a raw map byte in BL across a call to WORLDH, and the second instruction of WORLDH is mov bx,5. Every node at or above sea level came back as 5, which the colour ladder calls sea. Nodes below sea level made WORLDH return first, so they were right — which is exactly why it hid so well. A caller may assume what a routine returns. Never what it spares.

A SQUARE TURNED 45 DEGREES DOES NOT FIT

Twenty four nodes is 24,576 units along an axis, which fits in a word. At forty five degrees the corner of the square is 34,756, and a signed word stops at 32,767 — it reads −30,780, and the corner lands on the far side of the world. Those were slabs floating over the horizon, and turning found them because at 0, 90, 180 and 270 degrees nothing overflows. A view distance is the same in every direction, so the fix is the right shape: the first entries of a table already sorted by distance.

WHAT EACH CHANGE BOUGHT, MEASURED

Twenty four nodes, retrace wait off, one island, the aeroplane parked — one change at a time:

the square, as it was19 frames a second
the circle: a quarter fewer squares22
outcodes: a cull that is an AND of four bytes23
each edge drawn once, by the square on top26
three bytes of the engine overwritten with NOPs27

Look at the third line. The new cull is five times cheaper per square than the one it replaced, and it bought one frame: the old test was already throwing away the same squares. The time is in the squares that are drawn. Measure before you believe the instruction count. And at lesson 15's reach this runs at 76 frames — exactly what lesson 15's bare wireframe did.
